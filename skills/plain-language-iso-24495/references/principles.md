# ISO 24495-1:2023 — Principles and guidelines (reference)

This is the extracted normative content of ISO 24495-1:2023, plus practical notes for applying each guideline when writing a document.

## Definitions

- **Plain language** — communication in which wording, structure, and design are so clear that intended readers can easily find what they need, understand what they find, and use that information.
- **Reader** — anyone in the intended audience. Includes people who skim, people who use assistive technology, and people to whom the document is read aloud.
- **Author** — the person or team that creates or helps create the document.

If there are several audiences, the needs of the **primary audience** take priority when needs conflict.

## Principle 1: Readers get what they need (relevant)

### 5.1.2 Identify the readers

Identify who the readers are. Consider their:

- literacy and language skills;
- cultural backgrounds;
- subject-matter knowledge;
- accessibility needs and preferences;
- the languages they understand.

Provide the document in a language the readers know. That may mean complying with official language requirements or translating into other languages.

To identify them, use one or more of:

- previous communications with the readers;
- reader interviews;
- reader surveys;
- research and relevant literature.

### 5.1.3 Identify the readers' purpose

Why will readers read this document? Options include:

- following instructions to complete a task;
- deciding whether to do something;
- trying to understand a topic;
- finding out what the author wants them to do;
- learning about an area of interest;
- gaining knowledge for a specific purpose.

### 5.1.4 Identify the context

Consider:

- where readers will read the document;
- what technology they will use;
- how much time they will have;
- how long they will stay focused;
- how interested they will be;
- what their emotional state will be.

### 5.1.5 Select the document type

Match the document type to reader characteristics, purpose, and context. If a different format (video, form, dashboard) serves the readers better, use it instead.

### 5.1.6 Select content that readers need

- Put readers' needs first.
- Identify the questions readers need answered.
- Select content that fits their characteristics, purpose, context, and the document type.
- Leave out content readers do not need.
- Consider the author's own purposes and needs — but do not let them override reader needs.
- Select content ethically: be accurate, do not mislead, do not hide content readers need.

## Principle 2: Readers can easily find what they need (findable)

### 5.2.2 Structure the document for readers

Group related information together. Order it in a way that makes sense for readers:

- Place the most important message where readers can easily find it — usually the beginning.
- Introduce new information by building on what readers already know.
- For instructions or processes, use chronological order.
- Put information most readers need before information only some readers need.
- **If failing to follow a process can cause damage or harm, place warnings before the instructions.**

### 5.2.3 Use information design techniques

Show relationships visually:

- **Prominence** — make important elements stand out (larger font, bold, colour).
- **Proximity** — put related elements close together.
- **Similarity** — make elements with the same function look the same.

Also:

- Use white space to separate ideas.
- Use images, diagrams, tables, and lists when they make information easier to find.
- Keep visual style consistent throughout.

### 5.2.4 Use headings to help readers predict what comes next

- Write headings that describe what follows.
- Use a heading hierarchy that matches the document's logical hierarchy.
- Make headings scannable — a reader reading only the headings should grasp the argument.

### 5.2.5 Keep supplementary information separate

Content that only some readers need should be:

- clearly labelled as supplementary;
- placed after or apart from the main content;
- easy to skip.

Examples: appendices, footnotes, glossaries, detailed data tables, technical annexes.

## Principle 3: Readers can easily understand what they find (understandable)

### 5.3.2 Choose familiar words

- Use words readers already know.
- Prefer short, common words to long, rare ones.
- Use technical terms only when necessary and define them on first use.
- Use one term consistently for one concept. Do not use synonyms.
- Avoid idioms, clichés, and cultural references that non-native readers may not know.
- Avoid unexplained abbreviations and acronyms.

### 5.3.3 Write clear sentences

- Use active voice by default.
- Name the actor of the verb.
- Put the subject near the verb, and the verb near its object.
- Use positive statements. Prefer "you must arrive by 9 a.m." over "you must not arrive after 9 a.m."
- Use parallel construction in lists.
- Match the sentence structure to the idea.

### 5.3.4 Write concise sentences

- One idea per sentence.
- Average 15–20 words per sentence.
- Cut filler and hedges ("in order to" → "to"; "at this point in time" → "now").
- Cut redundant words ("advance planning" → "planning").
- Break long sentences into shorter ones.

### 5.3.5 Write clear and concise paragraphs

- One main idea per paragraph.
- Put the main point of the paragraph at the beginning.
- Keep paragraphs short — usually no more than 3–5 sentences.
- Use lists for parallel items instead of long runs of prose.

### 5.3.6 Consider including images and multimedia

Use images, diagrams, charts, tables, and multimedia when they:

- convey information more effectively than text;
- reinforce important information;
- help readers understand relationships, sequences, or comparisons.

Always caption or label images so their purpose is clear. Provide text alternatives for accessibility.

### 5.3.7 Project a respectful tone

- Address readers directly ("you").
- Use direct requests, not commands and not performative courtesy.
- Match register to the reading context. Internal company memos do not need "please" and "thank you" in every section — that reads as filler. Public-facing documents to strangers usually do.
- Do not condescend, blame, or lecture.
- Do not use humour that may exclude or offend.
- Assume readers are intelligent adults trying to accomplish something.

### 5.3.8 Ensure the document is cohesive

- Use consistent terminology, tone, and formatting.
- Link ideas with clear transitions.
- Cross-reference other sections instead of repeating content.
- Make sure the document reads as a single coherent piece, not a stitched-together set of drafts.

## Principle 4: Readers can easily use the information (usable)

### 5.4.2 Evaluate the document continually as it is developed

- Review each draft against the guidelines above.
- Ask peers to read it.
- Use readability tools as a rough guide, but do not rely on them alone.
- Revise as you learn.

### 5.4.3 Evaluate the document further with readers

Test with actual readers before publishing. Methods include:

- **Reader interviews** — ask a representative reader to walk through the document while thinking aloud.
- **Comprehension testing** — ask readers questions about the content to check understanding.
- **Usability testing** — ask readers to complete a task using the document.
- **Surveys** — ask a wider group about clarity, findability, and usefulness.

Focus on:

- Can readers find what they need?
- Do they understand what they find?
- Can they use it to make their decision or complete their task?

### 5.4.4 Continue to evaluate readers' use of the document

After publishing:

- Monitor how readers use the document.
- Collect feedback.
- Revise when new information, changes in context, or evidence of confusion appears.

---

## Applying the principles together

The four principles are interdependent. A document that is relevant but not findable is useless. A document that is findable and understandable but not usable fails its readers. Apply all four together, and evaluate against all four before publishing.
